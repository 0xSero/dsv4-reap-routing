We asked an AI which parts of its brain light up when it reads scripture

22 million tokens. Nine traditions. Every single routing decision, recorded.

The story, in four pictures
1. The expert that never reads the Bible · 2. Same faith, different wiring · 3. It "knows" Genesis 1:2 before it says it · 4. Harder text, more brainpower

Modern AI models are mixture-of-experts machines: inside every layer sit 256 small specialist networks, and for every single word it reads, the model picks 6 of them to do the thinking. Nobody fully understands how it chooses. So we did something simple and obsessive: we let DeepSeek-V4-Flash read scripture all day — the entire King James Bible, the Qur'an, the Bhagavad Gita, the Tao Te Ching, the Book of Mormon, and 1,267 books of Christian writing spanning 2,000 years — and we wrote down every choice it made. 43 layers × 256 experts × 22 million tokens. Then we looked for patterns.

1. The expert that never reads the Bible

One specialist, deep in the network (layer 42, expert #164), did something strange. Across all 1,189 chapters of the KJV Bible — more than a million tokens — it activated exactly zero times. Statistically it "should" have fired ~24,000 times. Then the Qur'an: it fires constantly. Christian theology books: constantly, at almost exactly the same rate. The Book of Mormon sits in between.

What this means: the model has the KJV memorised cold. When it reads the actual Bible, it's reciting from memory — that circuitry stays off. But when it reads something that sounds like scripture and isn't — a Qur'an translation, a Victorian sermon — a different part has to actually do the work of producing archaic religious English. The AI didn't learn "religion." It learned the difference between remembering and reasoning in a register.

The Book of Mormon's middle position is the detail we love: it imitates KJV style but isn't verbatim canon — and its rate lands neatly between "memorised" and "foreign."

2. Same faith, different wiring — but only in some layers

Here's a question a theologian would ask: does the model process the Bible and books about the Bible (sermons, commentaries, Aquinas, Augustine) the same way? On average — no. Christian literature turned out to be the Bible's least similar partner of all nine traditions, less alike than the Tao Te Ching. But the average hides the real story:

What this means: the network is a sandwich. Near the input (layers 0–2) it processes spelling and style — and there the two are almost opposites. In the middle (layers 3–6, 19) it processes meaning — God, covenant, sin, grace — and there Bible and doctrine strongly converge. Near the output (layers 39–42) it decides what voice to speak in, and they split again. Same God, shared meaning, different voice.

That's what memorisation looks like from the inside.

What this means: the more predictable the text, the fewer experts it takes. The Gita, essentially one long chant, runs on 26. Christian theology — 2,000 years of unpredictable arguments — needs 111. The router's "palette width" is a proxy for how surprised the model is, word after word.

So what did we learn?

An AI that has read everything still doesn't organise religion the way a comparative-religions department does — by tradition, by doctrine, by faith. It organises it by what the text is doing to it: am I reciting or computing? Is this the voice I know? How surprised should I be? The theology is in there, in the middle layers, shared across every tradition we tested. But the edges of the network belong to something simpler and stranger: style, memory, and the confidence of a mind that has heard these words ten thousand times before.
